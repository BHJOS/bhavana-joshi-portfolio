## The static mockup stopped being enough.

By the time my Figma library for this product had grown to several hundred
high-fidelity explorations, the questions the team needed answered had outgrown
what a picture of a screen can say. Does the capture flow still feel fast on the
third log of the day? Does a gauge animation read at a glance or does it
distract? What does a brand-new user see when every surface is empty? In early
2026 I moved my design practice into code. Working with Claude Code, I design by
describing intent, reviewing the running interface, and refining until the
behavior is right. The prototype, not the mockup, became the thing the team
gathers around.

## Context and my role

I am the Senior User Experience Designer on the flagship app of a health and
lifestyle nonprofit, an all-in-one product for continuous personal improvement.
It unifies health, habits, and goals through near-effortless data capture and
AI-powered insights. I joined in November 2025, working on a 12-person product
team and owning the Nutrition, Health, and Wellbeing surfaces. My remit runs
from research and user flows through high-fidelity UI to a working React and
TypeScript prototype that leadership, design, and engineering use to validate
real product behavior before committing production work. I began the engagement
in Figma and carried the strongest directions forward into code.

## A git-based design practice

Designing in code only earns trust if it is disciplined, so I run my design work
the way engineers run theirs. Every exploration lives in its own personal
worktree, so ideas can be tried and discarded without touching anything the team
relies on. Finished work goes up as a pull request the same day, and changes
move through tiered promotion gates for design, quality, and development before
they reach the main prototype. I also wrote Claude Code skill files for my core
components, short documents that encode each component's anatomy, states, and
rules, so every new screen built with AI assistance speaks the same design
language. The result is a prototype the team can trust, built through more than
170 commits across 350 plus files, with stakeholder review happening against
working screens the same day a change is made.

## One capture experience for seven kinds of life data

The app asks people to log almost everything about their day, and historically
each kind of data had its own flow. Meals, workouts, symptoms, mood, and the
rest were all captured differently, which meant seven behaviors to learn and
seven places for friction to hide. I unified them into a single capture
experience that opens from one button anywhere in the app. Every type shares the
same browse grammar. Previously logged items surface first as one-tap chips,
search works in place rather than on a separate screen, and anything new can be
created from the same sheet. Staged items collect in a tray, where each opens a
compact editor with the fields its type actually needs. A group of staged items
can be submitted together and saved as a reusable meal, recipe, or workout. The
flow shipped with full dark mode support and fresh-start empty states for new
users.

The design review was as important as the design. Before the build, I measured
the four proposed directions against the governing spec and documented twenty
gaps across interaction coverage, edge states, and accessibility. That review
set the build order, and the shipped flow closes those gaps in the design
system's own language rather than with one-off patches.

## Designing the AI features

AI features in the app had to earn their place by removing effort, not by adding
novelty. The clearest win is the meal photo scan. Point the camera at a plate,
and the flow identifies the foods, prefills the diary entry, and asks the user
to confirm instead of type. I designed and built this flow in the prototype, and
it was adopted into the team's main product surface. Alongside it I designed an
AI describe panel, where a person logs anything by saying it in their own words
and then confirms what the AI understood, and a scanner flow that turns labels
into structured entries. Designing these features meant designing for the
moments the AI is wrong. Every flow shows its inference in plain language, and
confirming or correcting it is a single tap.

## Clinical guidance made glanceable

Health data is only useful if it can be read at a glance without being misread.
For the Health and Wellbeing surfaces I designed gauge-style heroes for sleep,
mood, heart, and blood sugar, each opening into a summary that tells the fuller
story. For blood pressure I translated American Heart Association guidance into
colored zone banding, so a reading lands visibly inside a clinically meaningful
range instead of sitting beside an abstract number. The same principle carries
into assistive technology. Every chart in the system exposes a plain-language
text summary, so the glanceable version and the accessible version tell the same
story.

## Accessibility as system rules

Accessibility in a prototype is easy to postpone and expensive to retrofit, so I
made it part of the system's rules rather than a cleanup pass. I wrote and
maintain the project's WCAG 2.2 patterns document. It covers minimum touch
targets, contrast floors for secondary text, reduced-motion gating on every
animation, accessible titles on every sheet, and text alternatives for every
chart. Each pattern is recorded twice, once as implemented in the prototype and
once as its production equivalent, so the engineering team inherits decisions
instead of reverse-engineering them from prototype code.

## Results and where it stands

The prototype became the team's design source of truth. Leadership, design, and
engineering validate real product behavior in it before committing production
work, and stakeholder review happens against working screens rather than static
frames. The AI meal photo scan flow was adopted into the team's main product
surface. The accessibility patterns document carries every decision to the
production team in their own terms. And the practice itself may be the most
durable outcome. A designer's workflow of personal worktrees, same-day pull
requests, and promotion gates lets design move at the speed of conversation
while holding a quality line the whole team can see.
